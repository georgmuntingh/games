Pet Zoo tests

The clock's angle maths and 5-minute snapping, the hour hand's coupling to the minutes, the grading verdicts behind the gentle correction, the two-phase spaced-repetition schedule and its scheduler, tier unlocking, the micro-session caps and the reload-proof nap, the subject registry that lets one zoo teach more than one thing, the upgrade that carries an older save forward instead of discarding it, and the storage layer's refusal to throw. For maths: that the thirty-one rungs partition the deck so each one can actually be finished, that every difference has an addition fact to lean on, that the generated column sums are reproducible and really do produce the case they were asked for, that a skill cannot graduate until it has got every case inside it right, the verdict behind each correction — checked by running the mistaken procedure and feeding its output back to the grader — the ten-frame's filling and emptying, the column walkthrough's carries, and the answer strip's refusal to hint at how big the answer is. For the times tables: that a pair is one pet however it is written, that a missing factor is asked from both ends, that the six strategy rungs hold the pairs they were designed to, and that the array drawn over a wrong product really is the question as it was written. For multiplying with the numbers stacked: that the rows of the working are the two partial products and their total, that every step of the method agrees with the answer it comes to, that a forgotten shift is named rather than merely marked wrong, that every row has to be right, and that the walkthrough walks the one row that went wrong. For every method worked in columns: that the box being written names the two digits stacked over it and the carry coming into that column and nothing else, that a partial product names the multiplier digit its row belongs to and the multiplicand digit over the box once the row's own shift is allowed for, and that the last line names the partial products in its column — which, since the rows are not all the same width, is a different box in each of them. For dividing: that the hundred division facts are asked both ways round and partition their six strategy rungs, that a division leans on the product it is the flip side of, that the working comes out right for every question the eight method rungs can ask, that the boxes a child types into and the working the correction draws are laid out by one description rather than two, that no row of the stack runs off the page and no number is too long for the row it goes in, that the steps take the digits of the number being divided exactly once each and in order, that every row names exactly the numbers it is made from and no others — the two things the prompt lights up beside the row a child is writing in — that every mistake in the working is named by running it — and that a wrong method which happens to give the right answer is reported as a coincidence rather than as a mistake. For the eggs: that every pet's egg is stable and unlike its neighbour's in size, shape, pattern and colour at once, that a full zoo meets every one of them, and that not one speckle hangs off the side of a shell. For the weather: that a date always names the same sky, so two devices agree without syncing anything; that fair weather stays the rule and the rest the exception; that consecutive days are genuinely independent rather than walking through the table in order; that a rainbow only ever follows rain and stays rare; that yesterday is worked out by arithmetic rather than by subtracting a day, leap years and year ends included; that wet ground builds through the day and then holds, and is derived from the clock rather than remembered; that a habitat given no weather draws exactly what it drew before weather existed — the assertion every other habitat test quietly leans on; that no sky may darken a home the way night already has; and that the umbrella stands clear of the nest, the larder, the ball, the pet's own spot and everything the child has bought, in all 144 homes, while staying somewhere the pet can actually walk to. For the save: that renaming the subject carries a grown-up's choices across and does not pay a child twice for tiers they finished months ago, and that not one pet in an existing zoo changes species, name or colour under any of it. For handwriting: the stroke maths, the rasteriser's agreement with MNIST's own framing, the JavaScript forward pass against the numpy one that trained it, the reading of a backwards digit, and what the save keeps of a child's handwriting — and refuses to carry to anybody else's device. Rendering, dragging, the keypad and the writing pads are not covered here.
